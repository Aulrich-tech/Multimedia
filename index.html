Intro to Multimedia

How text, graphics, audio, video, and animation get combined, digitized, and delivered through a computer.

What multimedia actually is

Multi means many, media is the means of conveying information. Put together, multimedia is a computer-controlled, integrated delivery of multiple media elements — often with interactivity that lets the user influence what is presented, and when.

CombinationMultimedia
PacingUsually static, no timeline — read at any speedMix of static, dynamic, and interactive; has time-based media (audio, video)
SensesSingle sense (visual or auditory)Dual sense (visual and auditory)

The distinction matters: stacking a photo next to a paragraph is combination. Syncing narration to that photo, on a timeline the user can scrub, is multimedia.

The five basic elements

  • Text — titles, body copy, captions, menus. Typeface and spacing carry meaning: serif for printed material, sans serif for digital screens.
  • Graphics — still visuals: photos, icons, diagrams. Raster is made of bits and pixels; vector is built from math equations.
  • Audio — narration, music, effects. Adds emotion, emphasis, and accessibility.
  • Video — moving images from the real world with synced audio. The heaviest element to store and transmit.
  • Animation — computer-generated motion, drawn or rendered rather than filmed.

Hypertext and hypermedia

Hypertext is text linked non-sequentially — the clickable links that let you jump between passages instead of reading start to finish.

Hypermedia extends the same idea across every medium: images, audio, and video that themselves act as navigable links. The World Wide Web is the largest hypermedia system ever built.

Characteristics of multimedia

Combination

Integration

Digital

Components of a multimedia system

Hardware

  • Input / capture — cameras, mics, scanners, tablets, recorders
  • Processing — a capable CPU and GPU
  • Storage — large, fast storage (SSD or cloud); media files are big
  • Output — high-resolution displays, speakers, projectors

Software

  • Media-editing — tools for a specific medium: image, audio, video editors
  • Authoring / integration — combines elements into a finished, interactive product
  • Players and browsers — deliver the final experience to the audience

The multimedia production pipeline

  1. Analysis and planning — goal, audience, message, budget, platform
  2. Design — storyboards, wireframes, script, style guides
  3. Content production — create the raw assets: text, photos, audio, video, animation
  4. Integration — assemble the assets and add interactivity
  5. Testing — check it works, is accurate, and is usable across devices

Digital representation of media

Analog is continuous — it takes any value and varies smoothly, like a vinyl groove or a sound-level needle. Digital is discrete — it represents a finite set of numbers. Converting analog into digital is digitalization.

Why go digital? Perfect copies, precise editing, compression, network transmission, and compact storage — the reasons nearly all modern media are digital.

Bits, bytes, and binary

A bit holds a 0 or a 1 — the smallest unit of data. N bits give 2n possible values. A byte is eight bits. 1 KB = 1,000 bytes, 1 MB = 1,000 KB, 1 GB = 1,000 MB for estimates — strictly it's powers of 1,024.

Sampling and quantization

Sampling is measuring the signal at regular intervals. How often we measure is the sampling rate — samples/second for audio, pixel count for images.

Quantization is rounding each measurement to the nearest available level. How many levels exist is set by the bit depth: more bits, finer steps.

Nyquist Sampling Theorem: sample at least twice the highest frequency present.
Hearing tops near 20,000 Hz → CDs sample at 44,100 Hz, just over twice 20 kHz.

Digitizing text

ASCII: 7 bits → 128 characters — English letters, digits, punctuation. Unicode / UTF-8 covers virtually every writing system and emoji, using 1 to 4 bytes per character.

Each character maps to a number via a character-encoding scheme. Unlike other media, text is not sampled — it is simply looked up in a table.

Digitizing images (raster)

A raster image is a grid of pixels (picture elements). Two numbers describe its quality: resolution (pixel dimension, e.g. 1920×1080) and color depth (24-bit = 8 bits each for R, G, B = 16.7 million colors).

PPI/DPI describes how densely pixels are packed — the same image looks sharp on a phone but blocky on a billboard.

size = w × h × bit depth
= 1920 × 1080 × 24
= 49,766,400 bits ÷ 8 = 6,220,800 bytes
≈ 6.2 MB (uncompressed)

A JPEG of that same photo might only be ~1 MB.

Digitizing audio

  • Sample rate — samples per second (Hz)
  • Bit depth — levels per sample
  • Channels — 1 for mono, 2 for stereo
1 minute · CD quality: size = rate × depth × channels × sec
= 44,100 × 16 × 2 × 60 = 84,672,000 bits ÷ 8 = 10,584,000 bytes
≈ 10.6 MB / min

One minute of uncompressed CD-quality stereo already exceeds a full high-resolution photo — audio is the lighter of the two time-based media.

Digitizing video

Video is a rapid sequence of frames. Its quality — and its size — rest on four numbers: resolution (frame pixel dimension), frame rate (fps — commonly 24, 30, 60), and color depth (bits per pixel).

Uncompressed 1080p · 24-bit · 30 fps
per second = 1920 × 1080 × 24 × 30 ÷ 8
≈ 186.6 MB/s ≈ 11.2 GB/min

A whole movie would need thousands of gigabytes. Codecs like H.264/H.265 cut this by 100× or more.

RGB and CMYK

RGB mixes red, green, and blue light — each channel runs 0–255. Example: RGB(48, 84, 150) or hex #2E5496. CMYK mixes cyan, magenta, yellow, and black inks — the model used for printing.
