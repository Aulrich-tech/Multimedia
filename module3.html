Typography and Visuals

How letters are built and classified, the mechanics that give text hierarchy, and the color theory, design principles, and composition rules that hold a visual together.

Typeface vs. font

Typeface
The design of a set of characters — the shapes themselves. A font family, e.g. Barlow.
Font
One specific size, weight, and style of that typeface. E.g. Barlow Regular 44pt.

A typeface is the design; a font is one particular instance of it — a single weight and size you'd actually set on the page.

Anatomy of a letter

A large x-height is the best single predictor of whether small text will be readable.

Baseline
Invisible horizontal line on which most letters sit.
Cap height
Height of uppercase letters — baseline to the top of the capital line.
X-height
Height of lowercase letters.
Ascender
Part of lowercase letters that extends above the x-height, e.g. b, d, f, h, l, t.
Descender
Parts of lowercase letters that extend below the baseline, e.g. g, j, p, q, y.

Type mechanics

Font weight
Thickness of the strokes — light, regular, medium, or bold. The cheapest source of hierarchy.
Kerning
Adjustment of the space between two individual letters so they look visually balanced.
Tracking
Overall space between all the letters in a word, line, or block. Wide tracking is a film-title convention.
Leading
Vertical space between lines of text — tight, normal, or loose.
Point size
Size of the typeface. 1 point = 1/72 inch.

Titles, credits & hierarchy

Limited to two typefaces.

Legibility
How easily individual characters are distinguished — a property of the typeface.
Readability
How easily words and blocks are read — a property of how you set the type.
Hierarchy
Size, weight, colour, and position decide what is read first.

Titles / key art vs. on-screen text

Film titles / key artCredits / on-screen text
GoalSet genre and mood — memorable and distinctiveReadable quickly while scrolling

TechniqueWide tracking, custom effects, texture, few wordsConsistent size and spacing, stronger contrast with background
AvoidIllegible or clichéd font that fights the conceptToo small, low contrast, or too many typefaces at once

Color theory

Color is the fastest lever on mood — it works before the viewer reads a word.

Complementary
opposite hues, max contrast
Analogous
3+ neighboring hues
Triadic
3 hues evenly spaced
Complementary
Two colors directly opposite each other. Maximum contrast and energy — teal and orange is the blockbuster default.
Analogous
Three or more colors next to each other. Harmonious and calm, but low contrast — usually needs one deliberate accent.
Triadic
Three colors evenly spaced around the wheel. Vibrant and balanced, but hard to control — one color must dominate.

60-30-10 rule

A design guideline for distributing colors in a composition: 60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent. Most first drafts use three strong colors in equal amounts — that's the failure mode.

60% Dominant 30% Secondary 10%

Design principles — C.R.A.P.

PrincipleMeaning
CContrastCreating a noticeable difference between elements. If two elements are only slightly different, make them very different — or make one of them the same.
RRepetitionReusing visual elements consistently throughout the design: one color, one typeface, one spacing rhythm.
AAlignmentPositioning elements so they line up with each other — every element on one edge.
PProximityPlacing related elements together — how viewers know the credit block is one thing and the title is another.

Composition

Composition is the arrangement and organization of visual elements within a design or image — placement within the frame.

Rule of thirds
Key subject sits on the line of a 3×3 grid or at their intersections rather than dead centre.
Balance
Distribution of visual weight among elements. Symmetry is formal and stable (deliberately unsettling in horror); asymmetry is more dynamic but still has to feel settled, not accidental.
Focal point
Main area or element that attracts the viewer's attention first. A poster without one reads as noise.
Leading line
Line or visual element that guides the viewer's eye toward a particular area — roads, edges, and the direction of a gaze all do this.
Negative space
The empty or unoccupied area surrounding the main subject.

Gestalt principles

Gestalt explains how people naturally organize and interpret visual elements as a whole — why we see things as groups.

Proximity
Near things are seen as a group.
Similarity
Things that look alike are grouped — similar in color, shape, size, font, or texture.
Closure
The brain's tendency to complete an implied shape or image.
Continuity
Eyes naturally follow lines, curves, or patterns in a continuous direction.
Figure/ground
People naturally distinguish between a main figure and its background.

Critique block for a poster

  • Focal image — one dominant image the eye lands on first.
  • Clear title — one or two typefaces, legible at thumbnail size.
  • Deliberate palette — signals the genre, written down as HEX codes.
  • Negative space — room to breathe; not every gap filled.
  • Clean hierarchy — title, then tagline, then credits.
